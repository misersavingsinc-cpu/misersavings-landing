At some point in the middle of saving toward a goal, you hit a specific feeling. You know what you're working toward. You can see the number. You're close enough that it's real, but far enough that it still requires effort.

What happens next is more variable than most people expect.

## Two things that can happen when you're close

One thing that happens: you accelerate. The goal is visible. The finish line is close. The last stretch feels easier than the middle, even though the saving rate is the same, because the gap is small enough to feel manageable. You put in more effort, not less, as you get near.

This is sometimes called the goal gradient effect — the observation that effort tends to increase as a goal gets closer. It's reliable in some contexts: loyalty programs are designed around it, which is why a punch card that starts half-stamped gets filled faster than one starting from scratch.

The other thing that happens: you slow down. This is less discussed but equally real. For some people, being close to a goal produces a kind of pre-completion satisfaction. It feels, psychologically, a little like having already done it. The urgency that sustained effort in the middle of the save starts to fade precisely when you're nearly done.

Which one you experience depends on how you think about the goal and what it represents.

## What determines which way you go

The research on this tends to find that the goal gradient effect (acceleration near the finish) is stronger when the goal is defined as a specific achievement and when progress is tracked in a way that makes the gap salient.

If your goal is "save $1,000" and you can see that you have $820, the gap of $180 is small and concrete. That tends to produce acceleration.

If your goal is more abstract, "save more," "build up a cushion," or even a vague number, then being "close" is harder to feel, because you don't know exactly where the finish line is. In those cases, the near-finish satisfaction can arrive too early.

> A visible gap is motivating. A vague goal is not.

## What to do with this

The practical implication isn't complicated: the goals that keep you moving are the ones with a specific number and a visible current state.

"Save $500 for car repairs" with a balance counter you check occasionally is a better structure than "save for emergencies" with no specific target. The finish line needs to be something you can measure the distance to.

The second implication: the day you hit the goal is the day to set the next one, not the day to coast. The psychological satisfaction of completion is real and you should let yourself feel it. But the behavior that got you there, consistently putting money aside, is now a habit worth keeping, not a temporary effort to retire.

<div class="callout">
<strong>On the slump in the middle:</strong> the hardest part of saving toward a goal is usually not the beginning or the end. It's the middle, when you're far enough from the start that the initial motivation has faded but too far from the finish to feel the gradient pulling you forward. If you design for that slump — shorter milestones, an intermediate target, a visual progress marker — you increase the chances that the finish-line effect has something to work with.

## The version that actually compounds

The best savings goals have an intermediate milestone. Not just a final number, but a halfway point or a 75% point that's explicitly marked.

$500 saved is a win. $750 is a win. $1,000 is the goal. Structuring it this way gives you more finish lines to cross, which means more opportunities for the near-completion effect to kick in on your behalf.

The goal itself doesn't change. The way you experience the distance to it does. And that experience, it turns out, is most of what determines whether you get there.
