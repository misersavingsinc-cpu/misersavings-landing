"Three to six months of expenses."

If you don't have an emergency fund, that number is almost impossible to hold as a goal. Three to six months of rent alone, plus food, utilities, transportation, everything else, that's $8,000 to $20,000 for most households. Starting from zero, that number doesn't feel like a goal. It feels like a rebuke.

The advice is technically correct. Three to six months is the right target, eventually. But it's the wrong first step, and telling someone without an emergency fund that they need three to six months of expenses is a bit like telling someone learning to drive that they should get comfortable on the highway.

Here's what the first steps actually look like.

## What $500 actually does

The research on financial emergencies consistently shows that the most common ones aren't three-month disasters. They're $400 car repairs. They're $600 medical bills. They're the refrigerator that goes out, the broken phone that you need for work, the flight home for something unexpected.

A $500 emergency fund handles a meaningful percentage of real-world emergencies. It doesn't handle everything. But it handles enough that having it feels categorically different from having nothing.

Without any emergency fund, a $500 problem becomes a credit card problem. With $500 saved, it's an inconvenience you handle and replenish. That's a real difference, even though the goal is eventually much larger.

## The first milestone: $500

Before you think about months of expenses, think about $500.

$500 in 10 weeks is $50 a week. In 20 weeks it's $25 a week. In a month it might be a tax refund or a sold piece of furniture or a temporarily reduced expense. The specifics depend on where you are, but $500 is achievable for most people in a timeframe that's short enough to feel real.

Put it somewhere it earns something, a high-yield savings account or anything that isn't your checking account, and don't touch it until you need it for something that would otherwise go on a card.

Once you have $500, the next milestone is $1,000. Then one month of essential expenses. The three-to-six-month number becomes a third or fourth milestone, not the starting line.

## Why milestones matter more than the final number

A $15,000 goal with $200 saved doesn't feel like progress. It feels like failure. The denominator is so large that the progress is invisible.

A $500 goal with $200 saved is 40% of the way there. That's a different psychological situation. You have evidence that you're making progress and that the goal is achievable, because you're achieving it.

> The three-to-six-month target is right. It just shouldn't be the first thing you're measuring yourself against.

That's not a trick or a way of lowering your standards. It's the difference between a goal that motivates and a goal that paralyzes. The final number matters. So does the path to it.

## What to do with windfalls

If an unexpected amount arrives, a bonus, a tax refund, a gift, a freelance payment, the emergency fund should usually get some or all of it, before you decide what else to do with the money.

The reason is timing. A windfall arrives all at once, which is exactly when the emergency fund opportunity is clearest. Monthly saving builds the fund gradually. Windfalls can build it in jumps, which means you spend fewer months exposed to the full risk of having nothing.

<div class="callout">
<strong>On using the emergency fund:</strong> when you use it, that's what it's for. The goal afterward is to replenish it, not to feel bad about needing it. An emergency fund that got used is a fund that worked. Replenishment follows the same steps as the original build, just starting from whatever's left rather than from zero.
</div>

Three to six months of expenses is the destination. $500 is the first stop. The distance between them is real, but you don't have to see the whole path to start walking.
