Savings math gets presented as complicated. Compound interest formulas. Retirement calculators. Spreadsheets with multiple tabs. The implication is that understanding your savings requires precision.

Most of the time, it doesn't. A few rough numbers, held in your head, are enough to tell you whether what you're doing is working.

## The three numbers worth knowing

The first number is your monthly saving rate. Not precisely, not to the dollar, but roughly. Are you saving closer to $50 a month, $200 a month, or $500 a month? Those are different situations, and you should know which one you're in.

If you don't know your monthly saving rate, estimate it. Add up what's actually moved to savings in the last three months and divide by three. That's your number.

The second number is your goal and how far you are from it. If your goal is $1,000 and you have $300, you're 30% there. If your goal is $5,000 and you have $300, you're 6% there. Both of those tell you something. The first tells you you're close. The second tells you the goal needs to be broken into milestones.

The third number is roughly how long it takes to get there at your current rate. $500 to go, saving $100 a month: five months. $5,000 to go, saving $50 a month: a hundred months. That second number is a signal that either the savings rate needs to change or the timeline needs to be renegotiated.

## The mental math that matters

You don't need to know your exact balance to know whether you're on track. You need to know whether you're trending in the right direction, and approximately how fast.

| Saving | Goal | Approximate time |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| $50/month | $500 | 10 months |
| $100/month | $500 | 5 months |
| $50/month | $1,000 | 20 months |
| $100/month | $1,000 | 10 months |
| $200/month | $1,000 | 5 months |

The table isn't accounting for interest, and the math assumes consistent saving, neither of which is exactly true. But it's close enough to be useful. If your mental math says you should be there in five months and you're at month seven with half the balance, something is off. If you're ahead of the estimate, something is working.

> Precision doesn't help if you don't know roughly where you are. Rough math you actually do is better than precise math you don't.

## What the simple math tells you that a spreadsheet doesn't

A spreadsheet shows you where you are. The mental math tells you whether the current behavior is enough to get you where you want to go.

Those are different questions. A spreadsheet can show you a $2,300 balance and nothing about whether that's on track for your goal or not. The mental math, savings rate divided into remaining distance, tells you the answer in thirty seconds without opening anything.

The goal isn't to stop tracking. Knowing your actual balance matters. But the simple math is the decision-making layer, and you can run it in your head on a walk or while you're waiting for something. It doesn't require conditions. A spreadsheet does.

<div class="callout">
<strong>On interest:</strong> interest on a savings account adds a little on top of whatever's there, and the rate depends on your bank. Each percentage point is about $10 a year on a $1,000 balance. Real, but not enough to change the mental math meaningfully until balances get larger. Don't count on it to do the heavy lifting in the early stages.
</div>

The savings math that actually changes your behavior isn't the precise version. It's the version you can run in your head and act on. Three numbers, thirty seconds. That's usually enough to know whether what you're doing is working.
