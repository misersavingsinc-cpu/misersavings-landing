Why free trials keep costing money

November 10, 2026·3 min read·By Magdalena, co-founder and CEO

A free trial is a sales technique. That's not a criticism, just an accurate description. The product is free for a period, then it isn't, and the trial is designed to maximize the number of people who stay when the billing starts.

This is worth understanding concretely, because the gap between "I'll try this and cancel if I don't use it" and "I've been paying for this for eight months and forgot about it" is shorter than most people expect.

How the conversion is designed to work

The trial period starts at the moment of highest interest. You signed up because you wanted the thing. For the next two weeks or thirty days, you're engaged, using it, finding it useful.

Then the trial ends. Billing starts. You're slightly less engaged than you were on day one, which is normal, but you're still using it enough that canceling feels like a loss. So you don't.

Months pass. Usage drops. But the cost is small enough per month that noticing it on a bank statement doesn't trigger any particular action. It's just there. And the inertia of "I should cancel that" is harder to overcome than the inertia of "I'm already subscribed."

This is the model working as intended. The conversion from trial to paid subscriber happens not through a single decision but through a series of non-decisions.

What the aggregate looks like

Individual subscriptions are usually priced to stay below the "this is worth canceling" threshold. Eight dollars a month feels like nothing. So does twelve, and fourteen, and six.

The problem isn't any single subscription. It's that the accumulation of below-threshold subscriptions adds up to an amount that would be above threshold if you saw it as a single number.

Someone paying $8, $12, $6, $14, and $10 for things they barely use is paying $50 a month, or $600 a year, for the aggregate. Presented as $600/year, that's a number most people would react to. Distributed across five different line items and twelve months, it disappears.

The design is that you notice each charge individually, never the total. The total is the number that matters.

The friction that actually works

There are two reliable interventions, and they both involve doing something at signup rather than later.

The first: at the moment you start a trial, set a calendar reminder for two days before the trial ends. Not the day it ends. Two days before, so you have time to make a decision rather than notice you've already been charged.

The second: when you set up the reminder, write down why you signed up. "Wanted to try the meal planning feature" or "needed it for a project." Two weeks later, the specific reason is often already resolved or forgotten, which makes the cancellation decision much easier.

Neither of these works if you do them after the trial. The moment of signup is when you have the most information and the most motivation. That's when the commitment device is worth setting up.

On auditing what you already have: if you want to see what you're paying for, pull up the last three months of bank or credit card statements and look for recurring charges. Don't do this from memory — memory systematically underestimates what's there. The number of subscriptions most people actually have versus what they think they have is almost always different.

The version that requires less discipline

If the calendar-reminder approach sounds like something you'll intend to do and not do, there's a simpler version.

Don't sign up for trials that require a credit card and have no mechanism to try the thing without one. There are usually alternatives, free tiers, shorter commitments, one-time purchases, that don't start a billing clock.

The trial is optimized for the business. The version of the trial that's optimized for you is the one where you don't need to remember to cancel.
